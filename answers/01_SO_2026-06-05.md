## Ans. to the Q. No. 1 — Export Diversification in Bangladesh

### Export Diversification in Bangladesh: Moving Beyond a One-Product Economy

> "Don't put all your eggs in one basket." — an old proverb that now describes Bangladesh's biggest export risk.

**Introduction**
Bangladesh has become the world's second-largest garment exporter, but this success hides a structural weakness: readymade garments (RMG) earn about 80 percent of total export receipts. When the European Union, which buys the largest share of our garments, slows down, or when a price war breaks out among Asian suppliers, the whole economy feels the shock. With graduation from the LDC group now deferred to 24 November 2029, the next three years are a narrow window to build a broader export basket.

**Current picture of exports**

| Indicator | Latest figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Total exports, FY26 | USD 48.0 billion (−0.58%; target USD 55 bn) | EPB via TBS, Jul 2026 |
| Share of RMG in exports | about 80% | Bangladesh Bank, MPS H1FY27 |
| RMG exports, FY26 | USD 38.70 billion (−1.64%) | EPB via TBS, Jul 2026 |
| Exports, Jul–Oct FY27 | USD 17.04 billion (about +6%) | EPB via FE, Nov 2026 |
| US tariff on Bangladeshi goods | 10% (Section 301, since 24 Jul 2026) | Bonik Barta, 2026 |
| Import payments, Jul–Apr FY26 | USD 61.62 billion (+5.92%) | Bangladesh Bank, MPS H1FY27 |
| LDC graduation | deferred to 24 Nov 2029 | UN General Assembly, Oct 2026 |

**Why diversification is urgent**
1. **Concentration risk:** one product and two markets mean any shock hits 80 percent of earnings at once. The EU and the USA take 67.5 percent of RMG exports, and since July 2026 the US charges a 10 percent Section 301 tariff on Bangladeshi goods.
2. **Loss of preferences after 2029:** duty-free access under EU "Everything But Arms" ends after graduation. Under the new EU GSP Regulation applying from 1 January 2027, the apparel safeguard threshold falls to 37 percent, and GSP+ will require 32 international conventions instead of 27.
3. **Stagnant export growth:** FY26 exports of USD 48.0 billion fell short of the USD 55 billion target, while record remittances (USD 35.59 billion) rather than exports kept the external account stable.
4. **Jobs for the youth:** RMG is automating; new sectors are needed to absorb about two million new job seekers every year.

**Promising sectors beyond RMG**
- **Leather and footwear:** raw material is domestic; the Savar tannery estate needs a working central effluent treatment plant to win buyer certification.
- **Pharmaceuticals:** already export to more than 150 countries; the TRIPS waiver continues until graduation.
- **Light engineering, bicycles and electronics:** strong SME base and growing demand in Europe.
- **Agro-processing and frozen food:** jute goods, shrimp, vegetables and processed foods.
- **ICT and freelancing services:** low capital need, high value added.
- **Shipbuilding:** small ocean-going vessels for Europe and Africa.

**Main obstacles**
- Bonded-warehouse and cash-incentive facilities are designed mainly for RMG.
- Energy shortages and higher fuel prices raise production costs.
- Port congestion, long lead time and weak testing and certification facilities.
- Limited access to long-term finance for new exporters, and high interest rates (the policy rate was 10 percent until July 2026 and is now 9.5 percent).
- Weak branding and few trade agreements: the first EPA, with Japan, was signed only in February 2026, and none exists yet with the EU or ASEAN.

**Role of the banking sector**
Banks can lead diversification by: extending bonded-warehouse-style back-to-back LC facilities to non-RMG sectors; using Bangladesh Bank's Tk 3,000 crore window for non-traditional exports and the export development fund; offering export credit guarantees for first-time exporters; and financing compliance upgrades such as effluent treatment and green factories.

**Recommendations**
1. Give every export sector the same bonded-warehouse and duty-drawback facilities as RMG.
2. Ratify and use the Japan EPA quickly, and negotiate FTAs/CEPAs with ASEAN, India and the Gulf before 2029.
3. Meet the 32 GSP+ conventions on labour rights, human rights, environment and governance.
4. Build product-testing labs and international certification centres.
5. Ensure gas and electricity supply to economic zones.
6. Create a "Brand Bangladesh" campaign for non-garment products.

**Conclusion**
Export diversification is no longer a choice but a condition for sustaining growth after LDC graduation. If policy support, bank finance and infrastructure move together, Bangladesh can turn its manufacturing strength into a broad, resilient export economy.

### Impact of Fuel Price Hikes on the Economy of Bangladesh

> "Energy is the golden thread that connects economic growth, social equity and environmental sustainability." — Ban Ki-moon

**Introduction**
Fuel oil is the lifeblood of transport, irrigation and power generation in Bangladesh. Because the country imports almost all of its petroleum, every rise in world oil prices quickly reaches the farmer's irrigation pump, the bus fare and the market price of rice. In 2026 the war in the Middle East and disruption in the Strait of Hormuz pushed global energy prices up, and the government adjusted domestic prices twice.

**Recent price adjustments**

| Date | Diesel (Tk/litre) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Before April 2026 | 100 | — |
| April 2026 | 115 | +Tk 15, linked to global crude prices |
| September 2026 | 135 | +Tk 20 for all four fuels; petrol Tk 160 |

Source: Energy Division announcements reported by Dhaka Tribune and bdnews24, 2026. Diesel is about 66 percent of total fuel-oil use.

**Impact on the economy**
1. **Inflation:** transport and production costs pass into prices. Non-food inflation rose to 9.89 percent in October 2026, a 41-month high (BBS), even as food inflation eased.
2. **Agriculture:** diesel runs irrigation pumps and power tillers; higher costs reduce farm profit and can lower Boro rice output.
3. **Transport:** bus and truck fares rise, raising the cost of moving goods from farm to market.
4. **Industry and exports:** factories that use diesel generators face higher costs, making our exports less competitive.
5. **External sector:** import payments jumped 21.42 percent in April 2026 because of petroleum, LNG and fertiliser bills, putting pressure on foreign exchange reserves.
6. **Poverty:** fixed-income and poor households suffer most, because wage growth (7.63 percent) is lower than inflation.

**Positive side**
Price adjustment reduces the subsidy burden on the budget and the losses of the Bangladesh Petroleum Corporation, which was losing about Tk 89 per litre of diesel, and it discourages smuggling to neighbouring countries.

**Way forward**
- Introduce a transparent automatic pricing formula linked to world prices.
- Give targeted diesel subsidy to farmers during the irrigation season.
- Expand domestic gas exploration and long-term LNG contracts.
- Invest in solar irrigation pumps, rooftop solar and electric public transport.
- Monitor markets to stop traders from raising prices more than costs justify.

**Conclusion**
Fuel prices cannot be kept artificially low forever, but sudden hikes hurt growth and the poor. A gradual, formula-based pricing policy, protection for vulnerable groups and a faster shift to renewable energy are the keys to energy security.

## Ans. to the Q. No. 4 — Mathematics

**(a) Minimum on-season sale of oranges**
Loss in off-season = 1,800 × (11 − 8) = Tk 5,400
Overhead for 12 months = 12 × 3,000 = Tk 36,000
Profit per on-season orange = 10 − 6 = Tk 4
Let n = oranges sold in on-season. Overall profit ≥ 5,000:
4n − 5,400 − 36,000 ≥ 5,000
4n ≥ 46,400 ⇒ n ≥ 11,600
**Answer: at least 11,600 oranges.**

**(b) Speed of the stream**
Let stream speed = s km/h. Downstream speed = 10 + s, upstream = 10 − s.
Time is equal: 24/(10 + s) = 14/(10 − s)
240 − 24s = 140 + 14s ⇒ 38s = 100 ⇒ s = 50/19
**Answer: 50/19 ≈ 2.63 km/h.**

**(c) Number of staff**
Let staff = n and each bonus = x, so total = nx.
(n + 5)(x − 50) = nx ⇒ 5x − 50n = 250 ⇒ x − 10n = 50 … (i)
(n − 5)(x + 75) = nx ⇒ 75n − 5x = 375 ⇒ 15n − x = 75 … (ii)
Adding (i) and (ii): 5n = 125 ⇒ n = 25 (and x = Tk 300).
**Answer: 25 staff members.**

**(d) The two-digit number**
Let the number be 10a + b.
a + b + 7 = 3a ⇒ b = 2a − 7 … (i)
(10a + b) − 18 = 10b + a ⇒ a − b = 2 … (ii)
From (i) and (ii): a − (2a − 7) = 2 ⇒ a = 5, b = 3
Check: 5 + 3 + 7 = 15 = 3 × 5 ✓; 53 − 18 = 35 ✓
**Answer: 53.**

**(e) Pieces of wood**
Area of doors = 3 × 2 × 1.25 = 7.5 m²
Area of windows = 6 × 1 × 1.25 = 7.5 m²
Total = 15 m²; area of one piece = 5 × 0.60 = 3 m²
Pieces = 15 ÷ 3 = 5
**Answer: 5 pieces.**

## Ans. to the Q. No. 6 — Analytical Reasoning, Critical Thinking and Argumentative Writing

**(a) Interview schedule**
1. C must be on **Friday** and E on **Tuesday** (given).
2. E cannot be before A, so A must come before Tuesday ⇒ A is on **Monday**.
3. B cannot be on Monday or Friday, and D must be immediately after B. The only free consecutive days are Wednesday and Thursday ⇒ B on **Wednesday**, D on **Thursday**.
4. Check: A (Mon) is before C (Fri) ✓.

| Monday | Tuesday | Wednesday | Thursday | Friday |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | E | B | D | C |

**(b) Critique of the CEO's decision**
The CEO's conclusion is weak because it rests on one average and several hidden assumptions.
1. **Average delivery time is not efficiency.** It ignores cost per order, on-time rate, damage rate and customer satisfaction.
2. **Different conditions.** North may serve nearby urban customers while South serves remote areas; the 2-day vs 4-day gap may reflect distance, not performance.
3. **Averages hide variation.** South may deliver most orders in 2 days with a few very late ones that pull the average up.
4. **Capacity constraint.** Shifting 80 percent of volume to North may overload its staff, trucks and storage, so its delivery time will rise, possibly above 4 days.
5. **"Twice as efficient" is a misuse of ratio.** Halving time does not double efficiency in cost or quality terms.
6. **Geography.** Customers near South would face longer distances if served from North.

**Better decision:** compare cost per delivery, on-time percentage and capacity utilisation for similar customer zones; find why South is slower; then improve South or shift volume gradually through a pilot while monitoring results.

**(c) Implied assumption**
The economist assumes that **if deposits were not insured, depositors would be able to judge (and would actually take the trouble to judge) how secure a bank is, and would move their money accordingly**, so that this market discipline would force banks to be safer. In short, bank failures are at least partly caused by risky behaviour that depositor scrutiny could prevent.

**(d) Environmental protection vs economic growth**

*Arguments that environmental protection is more important*
1. Growth that destroys rivers, forests and air reduces future growth; environmental damage costs Bangladesh a large share of GDP through floods, salinity and pollution.
2. Bangladesh is among the most climate-vulnerable countries; protection is a matter of survival.
3. Health costs of pollution reduce labour productivity.
4. Global buyers and lenders now demand green compliance; protection keeps export markets open.

*Arguments that economic growth is more important*
1. A lower-middle-income country must create jobs for millions of young people and reduce poverty first.
2. Growth provides the revenue and technology needed to protect the environment later.
3. Strict rules may raise costs and drive away investment.

*Rebuttal and position*
The choice is false. Countries that grew first and cleaned up later paid a far higher cost, while green growth (renewable energy, green factories, sustainable finance) now creates jobs itself. Bangladesh already has more than 200 LEED-certified green garment factories, showing that environmental standards and export growth can go together.
**Position: Environmental protection should not be sacrificed for growth; the goal is green and sustainable growth**, supported by green banking, carbon pricing over time and enforcement of the Environment Conservation Act.
