# Likely Focus Writing Topics — Model Answers

> Each essay is about 500–600 words, about what you can write in 18 minutes. All figures are as of 19 November 2026, with sources. Memorise the data box and the sub-headings; the sentences can be your own.

Tip: Structure that scores: title → quotation → introduction with one fresh number → 5–7 sub-headings → one data box with source → recommendations → hopeful conclusion.

## Ans. to the Predicted Q. — Non-Performing Loans and Banking Sector Reform in Bangladesh

### Default Loans and the Road to Banking Sector Reform

> "A bank is only as strong as the trust of its depositors."

**Introduction**
Banks are the heart of an economy: they collect savings and pump credit into business. In Bangladesh that heart is weak. In June 2026, defaulted loans reached Tk 6.07 lakh crore, or 32.78 percent of all loans, which IMF data show is the highest ratio in the world. Restoring the health of banks is now the most urgent economic reform.

**The magnitude of the problem**

| Indicator | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Default loans, June 2026 | Tk 6.07 lakh crore (32.78%) | Bangladesh Bank via Prothom Alo |
| Default loans, Sept 2026 (latest) | Tk 5.97 lakh crore (32.17%) | Finance Minister in Parliament, 16 Nov 2026 |
| Private credit growth, Sept 2026 | 4.17% (record low) | Bangladesh Bank via Samakal |
| Default ratio, state-owned banks | 43.2% | Financial Express |
| Default ratio, Dec 2024 | 20.20% | Bangladesh Bank, MPS H1FY27 |
| Five merged Islamic banks | 84.22% of loans in default | 2026 reports |
| Money stuck in money loan courts | about Tk 6.48 lakh crore | Daily Sun, Sep 2026 |

**Causes**
1. **Political and insider lending:** loans approved on influence, not on business merit; some groups captured whole banks.
2. **Wilful default:** a culture where large borrowers expect rescheduling and write-offs.
3. **Weak supervision:** regulatory forbearance and repeated relaxation of loan-classification rules hid the real picture for years.
4. **Slow legal process:** money loan court cases drag on for years.
5. **Board-level governance failure:** directors interfering in credit decisions.

**Consequences**
Capital shortfalls and liquidity crises in weak banks; loss of depositor confidence; private-sector credit growth falling to a record-low 4.17 percent in September 2026; higher lending rates for honest borrowers; and pressure on the budget for recapitalisation (Tk 36,706 crore set aside in FY27 for bank restructuring).

**Reforms under way**
- **Bank Resolution Act 2026:** a legal framework to merge, restructure or close failing banks. Section 18A, which allowed former owners to reclaim banks, was repealed in September 2026.
- **Sammilito Islami Bank:** five troubled Shariah banks merged into one state-owned bank with Tk 20,000 crore government capital.
- **Deposit Protection Act 2026:** insurance coverage doubled from Tk 1 lakh to Tk 2 lakh per depositor.
- **Asset Quality Reviews** and a move to **Expected Credit Loss (IFRS 9)** provisioning by 2027.
- Recovery of laundered money: about Tk 70,446 crore of assets frozen or attached at home and abroad.

**Recommendations**
1. Publish a list of wilful defaulters and bar them from new credit, foreign travel and company directorships.
2. Set up special benches to settle large money-loan cases within a fixed time.
3. Pass the Distressed Asset Management Act to create an asset management company for bad loans.
4. Apply "fit and proper" tests to bank directors and limit family control of boards.
5. Give Bangladesh Bank full operational independence in supervision.
6. Merge banks only after independent asset-quality reviews and with fresh management, so that mergers do not spread the infection.

**Conclusion**
Bangladesh cannot reach its growth targets with a banking system in which one taka in every three lent is not being repaid. The new laws are a start, but their success depends on consistent enforcement, regardless of who the defaulter is.

## Ans. to the Predicted Q. — LDC Graduation Deferred to 2029: Opportunities and Challenges

### Using the Extra Three Years: Bangladesh's LDC Graduation Strategy

> "Graduation is not the end of the journey; it is the start of a new one."

**Introduction**
On 29 October 2026 the UN General Assembly deferred Bangladesh's graduation from the Least Developed Country (LDC) category from 24 November 2026 to 24 November 2029, granting the request of the new government. Nepal received the same extension. The decision gives Bangladesh three more years of trade preferences, but also three fewer excuses.

**Background**
Bangladesh met all three graduation criteria (per-capita income, human assets and economic vulnerability) in the 2018 and 2021 reviews. The government argued that the preparatory period was disrupted by the pandemic, the 2024 political transition and global shocks such as the Middle East war.

**Why the deferral helps**
1. **Preferences continue:** duty-free, quota-free access to the EU under "Everything But Arms" and preferential treatment in other markets remain until 2029.
2. **Pharmaceutical patents:** the TRIPS waiver lets local firms keep producing generic medicines.
3. **Concessional finance:** access to cheaper loans continues.
4. **Time to reform:** the economy is weak (FY27 growth forecasts 3.2–4.0 percent) and the banking sector is stressed. Graduating now could have added a fresh shock.

**Challenges that remain**
- Exports are concentrated: about 80 percent comes from RMG.
- The new EU GSP Regulation, from 1 January 2027, lowers the apparel safeguard threshold to 37 percent, while Bangladesh's share is about 50 percent. GSP+ will require 32 conventions.
- The US now charges a 10 percent Section 301 tariff on Bangladeshi goods.
- A UN assessment estimated export losses of more than USD 17.5 billion a year after graduation without preferences.
- Low revenue (about 7 percent of GDP) limits investment in skills and infrastructure.

**Opportunities to seize**
Better credit rating and branding as a graduating economy; more FDI (USD 1.78 billion in 2025, up 45 percent, according to UNCTAD); and a chance to negotiate new trade deals. The EPA with Japan, signed in February 2026, is the first such deal.

**What must be done before 2029**
1. Prepare to qualify for EU GSP+ by implementing labour, environment and human-rights conventions.
2. Sign FTAs or CEPAs with ASEAN, India, China and the Gulf, and ratify the Japan EPA.
3. Diversify exports: leather, pharmaceuticals, light engineering, agro-processing and ICT services.
4. Raise revenue through tax reform and digital tax administration.
5. Upgrade ports, power supply and logistics to cut lead time.
6. Upskill workers for higher-value manufacturing.

**Conclusion**
The deferral is a breathing space, not a holiday. If Bangladesh uses these three years for real reform, it will graduate in 2029 as a confident, competitive economy rather than a vulnerable one.

## Ans. to the Predicted Q. — Stagflation Risk: High Inflation, Slowing Growth and the Policy Response

### Between Inflation and Stagnation: Bangladesh's Policy Dilemma

> "Inflation is taxation without legislation." — Milton Friedman

**Introduction**
Bangladesh faces an uncomfortable mix: inflation has stayed above 8 percent for more than two years, while growth has slowed to its weakest level in decades. This combination of high prices and low growth, called stagflation, squeezes household incomes and discourages investment.

**The picture in numbers**

| Indicator | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation, October 2026 | 8.27% (food 6.40%, non-food 9.89%) | BBS |
| Wage growth, October 2026 | 7.63% (below inflation) | BBS |
| GDP growth, FY26 (revised) | 3.68% | BBS |
| FY27 growth forecasts | IMF 3.2%, World Bank 3.4%, ADB 4.0% | IMF, World Bank, ADB |
| Policy rate | 9.5% (cut from 10% in July 2026) | Bangladesh Bank |
| Poverty rate, 2026 | 22.5% (18.7% in 2022) | World Bank |

**Causes**
1. **Cost-push factors:** fuel price hikes (diesel up from Tk 100 to Tk 135 in 2026), LNG shortages and costly imports after the Iran war closed the Strait of Hormuz.
2. **Exchange-rate pass-through:** the taka's depreciation since 2022 raised import costs.
3. **Market syndicates and supply-chain weaknesses** in essential commodities.
4. **Investment drought:** high interest rates, weak banks, energy shortages and political uncertainty kept private credit growth near 5 percent.
5. **Government borrowing** from banks crowded out private credit.

**Policy response**
- **Monetary policy:** Bangladesh Bank kept the policy rate at 10 percent for almost two years. With food inflation falling, it cut the rate to 9.5 percent in July 2026 to revive credit, while holding it there in the first quarterly MPS.
- **Fiscal policy:** the FY27 budget (Tk 9.38 lakh crore) cut duties on essentials, introduced family and farmer cards, and kept the deficit at 3.6 percent of GDP.
- **Stimulus:** a Tk 60,000 crore refinance and pre-finance package targets CMSMEs, agriculture and closed factories, aiming at about 25 lakh jobs.
- **IMF talks:** a new USD 4.5–5 billion programme is being negotiated.

**Why tight money alone is not enough**
Most of today's inflation comes from costs and supply, not excess demand. Raising rates further would hurt investment without bringing down fuel, freight or food prices. Monetary and fiscal policy must act together.

**Recommendations**
1. Coordinate monetary and fiscal policy; avoid financing deficits through bank borrowing.
2. Break market syndicates through competition law and more importers.
3. Protect the poor with targeted cash transfers rather than blanket subsidies.
4. Restore energy supply through long-term LNG deals and renewables.
5. Clean up banks so that lower policy rates actually reach productive borrowers.

**Conclusion**
Escaping stagflation needs patience and coordination: steady disinflation through supply-side fixes, combined with targeted support to restart investment and jobs.

## Ans. to the Predicted Q. — Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Banking in Bangladesh

### AI in Banking: Opportunity, Risk and Responsibility

> "Banking is no longer somewhere you go, but something you do." — Brett King

**Introduction**
Artificial intelligence (AI) is changing how banks lend, serve customers and fight fraud. The IMF chief called AI "a slow-moving tsunami" that could affect about 40 percent of jobs worldwide. For Bangladeshi banks, AI is both a tool for efficiency and inclusion and a source of new risks.

**Uses of AI in banking**
1. **Credit scoring:** using transaction and mobile data to lend to farmers and small entrepreneurs who have no collateral or credit history.
2. **Fraud detection and AML:** machine learning flags suspicious transactions faster than manual checks, supporting BFIU's work against money laundering.
3. **Customer service:** chatbots and voice assistants in Bangla serve customers 24/7.
4. **e-KYC:** facial recognition with NID makes account opening quick.
5. **Risk management:** early-warning systems can predict loan default, which is vital when default loans exceed 32 percent.

**Bangladesh's digital foundation**

| Initiative | Status (2026) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Bangla QR | Mandatory single merchant QR since 1 Jul 2026; zero interchange fee from 1 Oct | Bangladesh Bank |
| Instant Interoperable Payment System | Targeted for June 2027 | Bangladesh Bank, MPS H1FY27 |
| Digital nano-loans | Refinance scheme extended to June 2028 | Bangladesh Bank, MPS H1FY27 |
| Digital bank licences | No final decision yet | 2026 reports |

**Risks and challenges**
- **Cyber security:** AI can also be used by criminals for deepfake fraud and phishing. The 2016 reserve heist shows the cost of weak security.
- **Bias and fairness:** algorithms trained on biased data may exclude women or rural borrowers.
- **Data privacy:** Bangladesh still needs a strong data-protection law.
- **Jobs:** routine back-office and teller jobs may decline. Global banks are cutting junior analyst intake, though S&P Global sees no near-term mass layoffs.
- **Skills gap:** few bankers are trained in data science.

**Recommendations**
1. Bangladesh Bank should issue AI governance guidelines on explainability, fairness and human oversight.
2. Enact a data-protection law and strengthen bank cyber-security standards.
3. Retrain staff for relationship management, data analysis and cyber security.
4. Use AI first for inclusion (credit for CMSMEs) and anti-fraud, not only for cost-cutting.
5. Use regulatory sandboxes, through the Regulatory FinTech Facilitation Office, to test innovations safely.

**Conclusion**
AI will not replace bankers, but bankers who use AI will replace those who do not. With sound regulation and training, AI can make Bangladesh's banks safer, faster and more inclusive.

## Ans. to the Predicted Q. — Remittance: Lifeline of the Economy and the Fight Against Hundi

### Record Remittances and the Fight Against Hundi

> "Remittance is the silent engine of Bangladesh's economy."

**Introduction**
More than a crore of Bangladeshis work abroad, and the money they send home has become the strongest pillar of the external sector. In FY26 remittances reached a record USD 35.59 billion, up 17.3 percent, while export earnings stagnated.

**Data box**

| Indicator | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Remittance, FY26 | USD 35.59 bn (+17.3%), record | Bangladesh Bank via FE |
| Remittance, FY27 to 16 Nov | USD 13.43 bn (+13.4%) | Bangladesh Bank via UNB |
| Exports, FY26 | USD 48.0 bn (−0.58%) | EPB via TBS |
| Gross reserves, 6 Nov 2026 | USD 34.85 bn (BPM6: 29.98 bn) | Bangladesh Bank via bdnews24 |

**Why remittance matters**
1. **Reserves and exchange rate:** remittances financed the trade gap and stabilised the taka around Tk 122–123 per dollar.
2. **Rural consumption and poverty reduction:** families spend on food, education, health and housing.
3. **Investment:** some remittance goes into small businesses, land and savings instruments.

**Why the surge happened**
A market-based exchange rate narrowed the gap between official and hundi rates. Stricter action against money laundering after 2024 and easier digital channels also helped.

**The hundi problem**
Hundi is an informal transfer system: the worker pays an agent abroad and the family receives taka at home, but no dollars enter the country. It drains reserves and is linked to money laundering and capital flight. A white paper estimated USD 234 billion was laundered from Bangladesh between 2009 and 2023.

**Challenges**
High migration costs, unskilled migrants earning low wages, dependence on the Gulf (now affected by war), and the risk that inflows slow down after the recent surge.

**Recommendations**
1. Keep the exchange rate market-based so that formal channels stay competitive.
2. Expand digital remittance through MFS and bank apps, with instant credit to family accounts.
3. Cut migration costs and fight fraudulent recruiting agents.
4. Send skilled workers (nurses, technicians, IT) to new markets such as Japan and Europe.
5. Offer remittance bonds and investment schemes for expatriates.
6. Let BFIU continue strict action against hundi networks.

**Conclusion**
Remittance has carried the economy through a difficult period. Making the formal channel the easiest and cheapest choice, and sending more skilled workers, will keep this lifeline strong.

## Ans. to the Predicted Q. (Bangla focus) — Climate Finance, COP31 and Bangladesh

Tip: Write this in Bangla in the exam. Bangla sub-headings: ভূমিকা · বাংলাদেশের জলবায়ু ঝুঁকি · COP31 ও বাংলাদেশের দাবি · লস অ্যান্ড ড্যামেজ তহবিল: প্রতিশ্রুতি বনাম বাস্তবতা · ঋণ নয়, অনুদান · গ্রিন ব্যাংকিং ও টেকসই অর্থায়ন · করণীয় · উপসংহার

### Climate Finance and Bangladesh: Expectations from COP31

**Introduction**
Bangladesh emits less than 0.5 percent of global greenhouse gases, yet it is among the countries hit hardest by climate change. At COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye (9–20 November 2026), Bangladesh's delegation, led by the Prime Minister, demanded fair and accessible climate finance.

**Bangladesh's climate burden**
Bangladesh estimates its annual climate losses at about USD 15.2 billion, from floods, cyclones, salinity, river erosion and heatwaves. Flash floods in 2026 damaged crops and pushed food prices up.

**Promise vs reality**

| Item | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Loss and Damage Fund | about USD 600 m received vs USD 2.8 bn requested by 119 countries | Climate Action Network, 15 Nov 2026 |
| Adaptation Fund pledges at COP31 | USD 50 m vs USD 300 m annual floor | Climate Action Network, 18 Nov 2026 |
| Global climate finance gap | up to USD 840 bn a year short of the USD 1.3 trn goal | UN News, 13 Nov 2026 |
| Renewable-energy climate finance to Bangladesh | 90.4% given as loans | UNB, Nov 2026 |

**Bangladesh's key demands**
1. Grants, not loans, for adaptation and loss and damage. Loans add debt to victims.
2. Simple, direct access to funds for vulnerable countries and local communities.
3. More finance for adaptation, not only mitigation.
4. Technology transfer for renewable energy.

**Role of banks: green banking**
Bangladesh Bank's sustainable finance policy requires banks to finance green projects (solar, energy-efficient factories, effluent treatment). Banks can issue green bonds and sukuk, price climate risk into lending, and expand refinance for solar irrigation.

**What Bangladesh must do at home**
Implement the National Adaptation Plan; build climate-resilient infrastructure; scale up renewable energy to reduce dependence on imported fuel; and improve transparency so that climate funds reach the people who need them.

**Conclusion**
Climate justice means the polluters pay. Bangladesh must keep pressing for grant-based finance while showing, through strong domestic action, that every dollar received is used well.

## Ans. to the Predicted Q. (Bangla focus) — Energy Security of Bangladesh amid the Middle East Crisis

Tip: Write this in Bangla in the exam. Bangla sub-headings: ভূমিকা · মধ্যপ্রাচ্য যুদ্ধ ও হরমুজ প্রণালী · আমদানিনির্ভর জ্বালানি খাত · গ্যাস ও বিদ্যুৎ সংকট (তথ্য-ছক) · অর্থনীতিতে প্রভাব · নবায়নযোগ্য জ্বালানির সম্ভাবনা · করণীয় · উপসংহার

### Energy Security of Bangladesh amid the Middle East Crisis

**Introduction**
Energy security means a reliable supply of energy at an affordable price. The war that began with US and Israeli strikes on Iran on 28 February 2026, and the near-closure of the Strait of Hormuz, has shown how fragile Bangladesh's import-dependent energy system is.

**The shock in numbers**

| Indicator | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Brent crude, 19 Nov 2026 | about USD 92/barrel (above USD 100 at the peak) | oilprice.com |
| Spot LNG price | USD 24–28/MMBtu (about USD 10 before the war) | bdnews24 |
| Gas supply vs demand | 2,624 vs about 3,800 mmcfd | bdnews24 |
| Rural load-shedding, August 2026 | 8–10 hours a day | 2026 reports |
| Diesel price | Tk 100 → Tk 135 per litre in 2026 | Dhaka Tribune |

**Impact**
A fire at the Moheshkhali floating LNG terminal on 21 July pushed load-shedding above 2,500 MW and hit about 2,500 factories. Irrigation costs rose, fuel and transport costs pushed non-food inflation near 10 percent, and the import bill strained reserves. QatarEnergy's force majeure halved LNG deliveries.

**Root causes of vulnerability**
Declining domestic gas fields, slow exploration, over-reliance on imported LNG and oil, and a slow shift to renewables.

**Way forward**
1. Intensify domestic gas exploration, onshore and offshore, through BAPEX and international partners.
2. Diversify LNG sources through long-term contracts with several suppliers.
3. Scale up solar (rooftop, irrigation, utility-scale) and wind.
4. Improve energy efficiency in industry and buildings.
5. Build strategic oil reserves for at least 60 days.
6. Use a transparent fuel-pricing formula with targeted support for farmers.

**Conclusion**
Energy security is national security. Bangladesh must turn this crisis into a push for domestic gas and renewable energy, so that the next global shock does not switch off its factories.

## Ans. to the Predicted Q. (Bangla focus) — Youth Unemployment and Skills Development

Tip: Write this in Bangla in the exam. Bangla sub-headings: ভূমিকা · তরুণ জনগোষ্ঠী: সম্ভাবনা ও ঝুঁকি · শিক্ষিত বেকারত্বের কারণ · চতুর্থ শিল্পবিপ্লব ও দক্ষতার ঘাটতি · কর্মসংস্থান সৃষ্টিতে ব্যাংকের ভূমিকা · করণীয় · উপসংহার

### Youth Unemployment and Skills Development in Bangladesh

**Introduction**
About two million young people enter Bangladesh's labour market every year. With growth slowing (FY26: 3.68 percent) and private investment weak, many educated youths cannot find suitable jobs. This is a "demographic dividend" at risk of becoming a burden.

**Causes**
1. **Skills mismatch:** degrees do not match what employers need, such as technical, digital and language skills.
2. **Slow job creation:** weak investment, energy shortages and the banking crisis limit new factories.
3. **Automation** in garments and services.
4. **Weak technical and vocational education (TVET).**
5. **Limited access to finance** for young entrepreneurs.

**Opportunities**
IT and freelancing, the care economy, agro-processing, light engineering, overseas employment of skilled workers, and the green economy (solar installation, recycling).

**Role of banks**
Collateral-free start-up loans; Bangladesh Bank's Tk 60,000 crore stimulus, which includes windows for youth employment and CMSMEs; the new venture-capital platform launched by banks (reported as Bangladesh Startup Investment Company); and digital nano-loans.

**Recommendations**
1. Reform the curriculum with industry participation; expand apprenticeships.
2. Make TVET attractive and of high quality.
3. Create a national job-matching platform.
4. Support start-ups through venture capital, incubators and easy registration.
5. Train youth in languages and certified skills for overseas jobs.

**Conclusion**
The youth led Bangladesh's political change in 2024; now they need an economy that can use their talent. Skills, finance and investment together can turn a jobless generation into the engine of growth.
